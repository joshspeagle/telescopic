# Coupling-based convergence diagnostics, practical tuning, failure modes and reporting for unbiased MCMC

Scope: notes for the design of `telescopic` (pure-Python unbiased MCMC with coupled chains, Jacob, O'Leary & Atchadé 2020). Literature covered through October 2026. Primary sources were read in full text where possible (arXiv PDFs converted to text; the published JRSSB article with its full discussion and reply, via the NSF PAR copy; the `LlagCouplings` and `unbiasedmcmc` code repositories, cloned read-only). Notation: `m` (Jacob et al. 2020; Biswas et al. 2019) and `ℓ` (Atchadé & Jacob 2024) denote the same "length" parameter; `N`, `R` and `C` are all used in the sources for the number of independent coupled pairs.

## 1. L-lag coupling bounds (Biswas, Jacob & Vanetti 2019): exact formulas, choice of L, variance of the estimates, plots, limitations, and the link to burn-in k

### Takeaway
For any lag L ≥ 1 and any t ≥ 0, `d_TV(π_t, π) ≤ E[max(0, ⌈(τ^(L) − L − t)/L⌉)]` and `d_W(π_t, π) ≤ E[Σ_{j=1}^{⌈(τ^(L) − L − t)/L⌉} d_X(X_{t+jL}, Y_{t+(j−1)L})]`. Both are estimated by averaging over independent coupled pairs. Larger L gives sharper (non-vacuous) bounds at roughly linear extra cost. The bounds are valid for any coupling and any π0, but they are loose, they describe convergence *from the chosen π0*, and their Monte Carlo estimates can be badly over-confident when rare, very long meetings exist (multimodality, heavy tails). The bound curve gives a direct burn-in rule: the smallest t at which the estimated bound drops below a tolerance ε.

### Cited Findings

#### Construction and definitions
- Algorithm 1 (sampling L-lag meeting times): draw X_0 ~ π0 and X_t | X_{t−1} ~ K(X_{t−1}, ·) for t = 1, …, L; draw Y_0 ~ π0; then for t > L draw (X_t, Y_{t−L}) | (X_{t−1}, Y_{t−L−1}) ~ K̄((X_{t−1}, Y_{t−L−1}), ·). Stop and return τ^(L) := t as soon as X_t = Y_{t−L}. The meeting time is `τ^(L) := inf{t > L : X_t = Y_{t−L}}`. K̄ must be a coupling of K with itself (K̄((x,y),(·,R^d)) = K(x,·), K̄((x,y),(R^d,·)) = K(y,·)), and the chains must be *faithful*: X_t = Y_{t−L} for all t ≥ τ^(L) — [Biswas, Jacob & Vanetti 2019, arXiv:1905.09971](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.09971)
- The bounds are stated for integral probability metrics `d_H(P,Q) := sup_{h∈H} |E_P[h] − E_Q[h]|` with a computable M_H such that `sup_{h∈H} |h(x) − h(y)| ≤ M_H(x,y)`. Total variation uses H = {h : sup|h| ≤ 1/2} and M_H = 1. The 1-Wasserstein distance uses H = {h : |h(x) − h(y)| ≤ d_X(x,y)} and M_H = d_X. The experiments use d_X(x,y) = ‖x − y‖_1, whose Lipschitz class "include[s] all first moments" — [Biswas et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.09971)
- Assumptions: (2.2) E[h(X_t)] → E_π[h] for all h ∈ H, and E[M_H(X_t, Y_{t−L})^{2+η}] ≤ D for all t ≥ L. (2.3) The meeting time has geometric ("sub-exponential") tails, bounded by Cδ^t for all t ≥ 0 with C < ∞ and δ ∈ (0,1); this "can be relaxed to allow for polynomial tails as in Middleton et al." (2.4) Faithfulness. For TV the moment part of (2.2) holds automatically. For W1 with the L1 metric it is equivalent to uniformly bounded (2+η)-th moments of the marginals — [Biswas et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.09971)

#### Exact bounds (with indices)
- **Theorem 2.5 (general IPM bound)**, for any L ≥ 1 and t ≥ 0: `d_H(π_t, π) ≤ E[ Σ_{j=1}^{⌈(τ^(L) − L − t)/L⌉} M_H(X_{t+jL}, Y_{t+(j−1)L}) ]`, with the sum set to zero when ⌈(τ^(L) − L − t)/L⌉ ≤ 0 — [Biswas et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.09971)
- **TV bound (eq. 4)**: `d_TV(π_t, π) ≤ E[ max(0, ⌈(τ^(L) − L − t)/L⌉) ]` — [Biswas et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.09971)
- **1-Wasserstein bound (eq. 5)**: `d_W(π_t, π) ≤ E[ Σ_{j=1}^{⌈(τ^(L) − L − t)/L⌉} d_X(X_{t+jL}, Y_{t+(j−1)L}) ]` — [Biswas et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.09971)
- Proof sketch: write π as the limit of π_t; apply the triangle inequality `d_H(π_t, π) ≤ Σ_{j≥1} d_H(π_{t+jL}, π_{t+(j−1)L}) ≤ Σ_{j≥1} E[M_H(X_{t+jL}, X_{t+(j−1)L})]`; replace X by the identically distributed lagged Y; terms with j > ⌈(τ^(L) − L − t)/L⌉ vanish by faithfulness. "Increasing L leads to sharper bounds through the use of fewer triangle inequalities" — [Biswas et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.09971)
- L = 1 special case (Jacob et al.): `d_TV(π_k, π) = ½ sup_{|h|≤1} |E[Σ_{t=k+1}^{τ−1} (h(X_t) − h(Y_{t−1}))]|`, hence `d_TV(π_k, π) ≤ min(1, E[max(0, τ − k − 1)])` with τ = inf{t ≥ 1 : X_t = Y_{t−1}} — [Jacob, O'Leary & Atchadé, arXiv:1708.03625](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.03625)
- Restatement in the 2024 review: `|π_k − π|_TV ≤ E[Σ_{j≥1} 1(X_{k+jL} ≠ Y_{k+(j−1)L})] = E[max(0, ⌈(τ − L − k)/L⌉)]` (eq. 3.2). The W1 version is written `|π_k − π|_{W1} ≤ E[Σ_{j=1}^{⌊(τ−k−1)/L⌋} |X_{k+jL} − Y_{k+(j−1)L}|]` (eq. 3.4). In the review τ := inf{t ≥ L : X_t = Y_{t−L}}. The review also notes that the p-th power of the p-Wasserstein distance can be bounded the same way (Papp & Sherlock 2022a) — [Atchadé & Jacob 2024, arXiv:2406.06851](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.06851)
- Closed form under geometric meeting times: if δ = τ − (L − 1) ~ Geometric(p) and q = 1 − p, then `d_TV(π_k, π) ≤ E[J_{k,L}] = q^{k+1}/(1 − q^L)`, with `J_{k,L} = max(0, ⌈(τ_L − L − k)/L⌉)`. Craiu & Meng note that "the bound can be trivial, because it can be larger than one for small k and/or L" — [Craiu & Meng, arXiv:2008.12662](https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.12662)
- **Improved TV bound using the same meeting times (Craiu & Meng, "Double Happiness", Statistica Sinica 2022)**: `B_{k,L} = Σ_{j≥1} min{P(J ≥ j), P(J ≤ j)}`, equivalently `B_{k,L} = E|J − m_J| + P(J > 0) − S_{k,L}` (m_J the smallest integer median; S_{k,L} = max{P(J > m_J), P(J < m_J)} ≤ 0.5), or `0.5 Σ_{j≥1}[1 − |P(τ > k+(j+1)L) + P(τ > k+jL) − 1|] + 0.5 P(τ > k+L)`. It never exceeds E[J], and it equals E[J] if and only if 2p_0 ≥ 1 − p_1 (p_i = P(J = i)). The improvement matters when t is small or τ is large, i.e. when mixing is poor — [Craiu & Meng, arXiv:2008.12662](https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.12662). The abstract adds that "gains diminish as L increases" — [arXiv abstract page](https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.12662)
- SMC/self-normalised IS bias via coupled PIMH (t = 0): `d_TV(q^(N), π) ≤ E[max(0, ⌈(τ^(L) − L)/L⌉)] = E[(1 − α(Ẑ_{L−1})) / (1 − (1 − α(Ẑ_{L−1}))^L)]`, where α(Ẑ) is the PIMH acceptance probability from a state with normalising-constant estimate Ẑ — [Biswas et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.09971)

#### Reference implementation (R code)
- `tv_upper_bound_estimates(coupling_times, L, t)` returns `pmax(0, ceiling((coupling_times − L − t)/L))`, which is averaged over runs. `simulate_meeting_time` returns `meeting_time = Inf` if `max_iterations` is reached, and compares states with `all(chain_state1 == chain_state2)` by default (the comparator is configurable) — [LlagCouplings: tv_wasserstein_bounds.R](https://github.com/niloyb/LlagCouplings/blob/master/tv_wasserstein_bounds.R), [coupling.R](https://github.com/niloyb/LlagCouplings/blob/master/coupling.R)
- The Wasserstein/IPM bound is accumulated online. After each coupled step at time t, the distance M(X_t, Y_{t−L}) is added to the bound at every index t − jL ≥ 0 (j ≥ 1), so the trajectories need not be stored — [LlagCouplings: coupling.R (`simulate_ipm_bounds`)](https://github.com/niloyb/LlagCouplings/blob/master/coupling.R)
- The empirical TV bound `C^{-1} Σ_c max(0, ⌈(τ_c − L − k)/L⌉)` "is exactly zero for all k ≥ max_c τ_c − L", so it only needs evaluating for k < max_c τ_c − L — [Atchadé & Jacob 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.06851)

#### Choosing L
- Cost and sharpness: getting τ^(L) costs L draws from K plus τ^(L) − L draws from K̄. Once L is large enough that X_L is effectively stationary, the distribution of τ^(L) − L depends only on K̄ and the cost grows linearly in L. For small L the K̄ draws dominate, so increasing L "might not significantly impact the cost until the distribution of τ^(L) − L becomes stable in L". Recommended strategy: "starting with L = 1, plotting the bounds… and increasing L until the estimated upper bound for d_TV(π_0, π) is close to 1". The benefits eventually diminish because the coupling is not optimal (Thorisson 1986) — [Biswas et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.09971)
- Review rule: "first generate meeting times with L = 1, and then redefine L as a large (e.g. 99%) empirical quantile of τ − L, the number of coupled transitions leading to the meeting time." Their Figure 6 shows TV and W1 bounds for lags 5, 50 and 250, each from 10^4 meeting times, on log-scale y-axes — [Atchadé & Jacob 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.06851)
- A 2025 paper summarises the published handbook chapter (Atchadé & Jacob 2026, *Handbook of MCMC* 2nd ed., ch. 17, Sec. 3) as choosing L "adaptively as a statistic of the meeting time τ, for instance the 95% percentile of meeting times thus observed for a previous choice of L". It describes calibrating L as "an adaptive offline procedure" — [Corenflos & Dau, arXiv:2510.07559](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.07559)
- Phylogenetics practice: "we run multiple pairs of chains at an increasing sequence of lags until the estimated bounds stabilise". Sampling from the marginal kernel is cheaper than from the coupled kernel, "so the computational cost of increasing the lag is relatively minor but can greatly improve estimates of the TV bound" — [Kelly, Ryder & Clarté 2023, arXiv:2108.13328](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.13328)
- High-dimensional regression: lags L = 750 (maize GWAS) and L = 200 (riboflavin) "were selected based on some preliminary runs… which incurs an additional preliminary cost. Any choice of lag results in valid upper bounds, but only large enough lags lead to upper bounds that are in the interval [0, 1] even for small iterations t" — [Biswas, Bhattacharya, Jacob & Johndrow 2022, arXiv:2012.04798](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.04798)
- Lags used in published examples: L ∈ {1, 150} (N(0,1) target, N = 10,000 runs); L ∈ {1, 18,000} (bimodal); L = 10^6 for single-site Gibbs (N = 500) and L = 2×10^4 for parallel tempering on a 32×32 Ising model at β = 0.46 — [Biswas et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.09971). Code: HMC lags 500–2000 and Pólya-Gamma lag 350, with 100 runs each — [LlagCouplings logistic_regression scripts](https://github.com/niloyb/LlagCouplings/tree/master/logistic_regression)

#### Variance and estimation error of the bound estimates
- Bimodal target π = ½N(−4,1) + ½N(4,1), RWMH with σ = 1 and π0 = N(10,1): with L = 1 the bound from N = 1000 runs differs greatly from N = 5000 or 10,000. Over 1000 experiments the 1-lag meetings "always occurred quickly in the mode nearest to the initial distribution", whereas the larger N included runs where one chain jumped modes before meeting. "Thus the results obtained with N = 1000 repeats can be misleading… empirical averages… are not guaranteed to be accurate after any fixed number N of repeats." With L = 18,000 the bounds for different N coincide, "which suggests that the variance of the estimators is small" — [Biswas et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.09971)
- The bounds depend on the coupling ("poor couplings give poor bounds", although the true TV does not), and "if τ has large variance, the bounds can be dramatically over-confident" — [Statisfaction blog (P. Jacob), 2019](https://statisfaction.wordpress.com/2019/06/05/estimating-convergence-of-markov-chains/)
- "Each meeting time is sampled independently so we could also resample them to estimate the uncertainty in the TV bound" (bootstrap) — [Kelly et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.13328)
- The variability of τ affects the bound itself, not only its estimate: "not only the average coupling time matters, but the variation of the coupling time is important too" — [Craiu & Meng](https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.12662)

#### How the plots are made
- Biswas et al.: Fig. 1 shows the marginal distributions of the chain over time (ridgeline plots via ggridges), then the TV and W1 bound curves against iteration for L = 1 and L = 150 (N = 10,000), with exact distances overlaid. Fig. 2 uses a log-x axis (iterations 10 to 10^4) for the bimodal case and overlays N ∈ {1000, 5000, 10,000}. Fig. 3 (Ising) uses a log-x axis up to 10^6. Fig. 4 compares Pólya-Gamma Gibbs with HMC at several step counts. Fig. 5 plots the estimated mixing-time bound t_mix(0.25) against dimension on a log-y axis — [Biswas et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.09971)
- Kelly et al. plot 1 − ECDF of τ^(l) on a log scale to check that the tails "decay geometrically for each lag", next to bound curves for several lags and the field-standard ASDSF diagnostic — [Kelly et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.13328)

#### Limitations
- Looseness: "The bounds are not tight, in part due to the couplings not being maximal… but experiments suggest that they can be practical." "Since the proposed upper bounds are not tight, the true convergence rates of the Markov chains under consideration may be ordered differently" — [Biswas et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.09971)
- A later erratum to Biswas et al. Fig. 5, posted in the code repository (date not verified), repeats the point that "they are simply upper bounds, so the actual mixing times could be ordered differently" — [LlagCouplings fig5_erratum.tex](https://github.com/niloyb/LlagCouplings/blob/master/ula_versus_mala/fig5_erratum.tex)
- Dependence on π0: unlike Doeblin's coupling inequality |π_k − π|_TV ≤ P(τ > k), which needs one chain started from π, the L-lag bounds "involve pairs of chains started from an arbitrary π0". Limitation: with a multimodal π, sticky chains and π0 concentrated in one local mode, "the user might then observe a small empirical average of the meeting time, even after many independent runs. Yet the expectation… could be much larger." The risk "is mitigated by specifying an initial distribution π0 that is spread out relative to the modes of π, or by increasing the lag L" — [Atchadé & Jacob 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.06851)
- The bound is for the full (possibly extended) state. For parallel tempering "the targets are different… as PT operates on an extended space", and a meeting needs all C pairs of tempered chains to meet, a trade-off when adding chains. For marginals, "the total variation distance between some marginals of two multivariate distributions is less than the total variation distance between the joint distributions" — [Biswas et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.09971)
- Diagnostics only: knowing ‖μ_t − π‖_TV < ε "does not allow us to translate perfect knowledge of μ_t to perfect knowledge of π"; the L-lag method is "useful as an alert tool… or as a comparison tool" — [Corenflos & Dau 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.07559)

#### From bounds to burn-in k
- Uses listed in the paper: "(i) determining MCMC burn-in, (ii) comparing different MCMC algorithms…, (iii) comparing exact and approximate MCMC". The mixing-time estimate is `t̂_mix(ε) = inf{k ≥ 0 : Ê[max(0, ⌈(τ^(L) − L − k)/L⌉)] < ε}`, with ε = 0.25 in the ULA/MALA study — [Biswas et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.09971)
- GWAS regression with (n, p) ≈ (2000, 100,000): "a burn-in of just 700 iterations can suffice". The maize and riboflavin chains converge "in less than 1000 and 500 iterations respectively" (100 coupled chains) — [Biswas et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.04798)
- For the estimator, "the bias cancellation is exactly zero in the event {τ − L < k}. By setting k as a large quantile of τ − L, we ensure that the event occurs with high probability" — [Atchadé & Jacob 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.06851)
- Analytic burn-in versus coupling: for the baseball Gibbs sampler Rosenthal's proven burn-in is 140 iterations. With unbiased MCMC, k = 3 and m = 30 already recover MCMC efficiency (cost about 33 MCMC steps), "a strong indication that this recommended burn-in is conservative" — [Jacob et al., arXiv:1708.03625](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.03625)

### Inferences
- **Identity linking bound and tail probabilities** (my derivation, checked numerically; consistent with Craiu & Meng's eq. 2.13, where {J > j} = {τ > k + (j+1)L}): `E[max(0, ⌈(τ − L − k)/L⌉)] = Σ_{j≥1} P(τ > k + jL)`. When L is large enough that P(τ > k + 2L) ≈ 0, the TV bound at k reduces to `P(τ − L > k)`. The two rules from the literature then coincide: "k = 99% quantile of τ − L" means "estimated TV bound ≈ 0.01 at k". `telescopic` can use one quantity (the empirical tail of τ − L) for both the burn-in diagnostic and the choice of k.
- The upper limits in Biswas et al. (⌈(τ−L−t)/L⌉) and in the review's W1 bound (⌊(τ−t−1)/L⌋) are the same integer whenever τ − t ≥ 1. I checked this identity exhaustively for τ < 300, L < 20.
- To turn bounds into bias statements for users: with the ½-sup convention, |E h(X_t) − π(h)| ≤ 2 sup|h| · d_TV(π_t, π) for bounded h, and ≤ Lip(h) · W1 for Lipschitz h. The TV distance to stationarity of a Markov chain is non-increasing in t, so the bound at the first retained iteration also bounds every later iteration. These are standard facts, not taken from the sources above.
- Report `min(1, Ê[J])`, since the raw estimate can exceed 1. With large L, J ∈ {0,1} almost always, so the estimator is a binomial proportion with SE ≤ 0.5/√N. When the estimate is exactly 0 (any t ≥ max τ − L), report a one-sided upper confidence bound (about 3/N at 95% if J ∈ {0,1}) rather than 0.
- In my synthetic check the Craiu–Meng bound reduced E[J] from 39.9 to 25.4 at L = 1, k = 0 and was identical to E[J] at L = 25. It is cheap (computed from the same meeting times) but mostly redundant once L is set large, as recommended. It is a plug-in of a nonlinear functional, so it is consistent but not an unbiased estimate of B_{k,L}.
- The W1 bound needs a metric on the user's state, and its value depends on parameter scaling. Default to the TV bound (metric-free, which suits phylogenetics and discrete states). Offer W1 only with an explicit metric, for example L1 on standardised coordinates.

### Gaps
- My text extraction of the exact normalisation in Biswas et al.'s Assumption 2.3 was garbled. The notes give its form (geometric tails, Cδ^t) but not the exact expression.
- No source gives a principled rule for N (the number of pairs) for the bound, or a non-asymptotic confidence statement for the estimated bound. Beyond the CLT and a passing mention of bootstrapping, uncertainty quantification of the bound is ad hoc in the literature.
- No source treats censored meeting times (pairs stopped at a cap) inside the bound estimator. The reference code treats them as τ = ∞, which gives an infinite bound.

## 2. Earlier and related coupling diagnostics, applications, and 2024–2026 follow-ups

### Takeaway
Johnson (1996, 1998) introduced coupled-chain diagnostics: common random numbers, several chains, and γ- (maximal) couplings of MH proposals. His bounds depend on an often-unknown rejection-sampling constant. Biswas et al. (2019) removed that constant with lagged pairs. Since then the main developments are: tailored couplings that make the bounds usable in high dimension (Biswas et al. 2022 for Gibbs, Papp & Sherlock 2025 for RWM); real applications (phylogenetics 2023; GWAS regression 2022); variance reductions of the bound (Craiu & Meng 2022); non-coupling Wasserstein bounds from independent replicates (Papp & Sherlock, 2022–2025); CRN-based Wasserstein bounds (Sixta, Rosenthal & Brown 2023–2026); f-divergence bounds from interacting chains (Corenflos & Dau 2025–2026); and multi-marginal couplings (NeurIPS 2026). I found no applications of coupling diagnostics in astronomy or ecology. The physics examples are Ising-type benchmarks.

### Cited Findings

#### Johnson (1996, 1998) and other early coupling diagnostics
- Johnson (1996), as summarised by Biswas et al.: c ≥ 2 chains start from π0 and evolve jointly (no lag) so that all coincide after a random time T_c. If a draw from π0 would be accepted as a draw from π by a rejection sampler with probability 1 − r, then `d_TV(π_t, π) ≤ P(T_c > t) × (1 − r^c)^{-1}`. The limitation is "its reliance on the quantity r, which might be unknown or very close to one in challenging settings". The tuning parameter is c, the number of chains, rather than a lag — [Biswas et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.09971)
- Johnson's Theorem 2 as restated by Sixta et al.: with τ = min{n : d(X_n, Y_n) ≤ ε}, `‖π − L(X_n)‖_TV ≤ 2K_1(π,ν)P(τ ≥ n) + O(ε)`, where K_1 = 1/(1 − r(π,ν)) = ess sup f_π/f_ν. They note that exact coalescence d = 0 was "not realistically attainable" in their CRN simulations, which is why Johnson needs ε — [Sixta, Rosenthal & Brown, arXiv:2309.15735](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.15735)
- Neal's critique of Johnson (1996), which runs chains from overdispersed starts with the *same* random numbers: "using the states immediately following the time when all chains coalesce introduces a bias… favouring states where coalescence is more likely". Also, a single random-number sequence "could… happen to produce atypically fast coalescence" — [Neal 1999, "Circularly-coupled Markov chain sampling", arXiv:1711.04399](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.04399)
- Johnson (1998) introduced the coupling that unbiased MCMC uses for MH: a maximal coupling (which he calls "γ-coupling") of the two proposal distributions plus a common uniform for accept/reject — [Jacob et al., arXiv:1708.03625](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.03625); [Atchadé & Jacob 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.06851)
- Cowles & Rosenthal (1998) numerically approximated the constants in drift/minorisation bounds. Biswas et al. cite this as one of the few prior computable-bound approaches, alongside Johnson — [Biswas et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.09971)

#### Coupling-based assessment for Gibbs samplers in high-dimensional regression (Biswas, Bhattacharya, Jacob & Johndrow, JRSSB 84(3):973–996, 2022)
- Goal: non-asymptotic stopping criteria. Standard ESS and R̂ "rely on the asymptotic behavior… and thus effectively ignore the non-asymptotic 'burn-in' bias". In GWAS-scale problems MCMC "can require seconds or minutes per iteration" — [Biswas et al. 2022, arXiv:2012.04798](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.04798); journal details from [Atchadé & Jacob 2024 references](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.06851)
- One-scale (maximal) coupling: its meeting times have provably exponential tails, but empirically "increase exponentially and have heavier tails" as p grows. The two-scale coupling uses maximal couplings only when a metric d(C_t, C̃_t) is below a threshold d0 (d0 = 0.5 used) and common random numbers otherwise. It gave "orders of magnitude reductions" in meeting times. With d0 = 0 (CRN always), "the chains… may come arbitrarily close but will never exactly meet" — [Biswas et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.04798)
- Results: TV bounds from 100 coupled chains, lags 750 (maize, n = 2266, p = 98,385) and 200 (riboflavin, n = 71, p = 4088). Maize took about 60 s per coupled iteration, "running one coupled chain until meeting can take more than one day"; riboflavin took 10–20 s per meeting. Half-t(ν) priors with ν > 1 give "orders of magnitude computational improvements" over the Horseshoe — [Biswas et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.04798)

#### Phylogenetics application (Kelly, Ryder & Clarté, Annals of Applied Statistics 17(2):1419–1443, 2023)
- A contractive coupling of MH over tree topologies, node ages, scalar parameters and latent variables (catastrophes, missing data) diagnoses convergence "jointly across all components of the phylogenetic model on trees with up to 200 leaves" — [Kelly et al., arXiv:2108.13328](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.13328); citation from [Project Euclid](https://projecteuclid.org/journals/annals-of-applied-statistics/volume-17/issue-2/Lagged-couplings-diagnose-Markov-chain-Monte-Carlo-phylogenetic-inference/10.1214/22-AOAS1676.short)
- The TV bound is "particularly attractive for phylogenetic inference problems as it does not require a metric on the space… of trees". "That pairs of chains meet and do not separate is a strong validation of our software implementation" — [Kelly et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.13328)
- Compared with ASDSF (MrBayes' average standard deviation of split frequencies, threshold 0.01), the TV bound sometimes diagnosed convergence earlier (8 taxa). In a real-data example the bounds were negligible within 2.5×10^5–5×10^5 iterations, "in contrast, ASDSF fails to decay below the arbitrary threshold of 0.01 within 10^6 iterations". With 16 taxa the TV bounds were slow while ASDSF looked fine, "more likely… because ASDSF is not diagnosing convergence on the other components of the model". Large trees used 25 pairs at one lag (10^5 for 100 leaves, 5×10^5 for 200) — [Kelly et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.13328)

#### Other applications (including physics-like benchmarks)
- Ising model: single-site Gibbs shows a plateau in the TV bound, while parallel tempering converges faster ("consistent with theoretical work on faster mixing times of PT"). Other uses: Pólya-Gamma Gibbs versus HMC on a 49-dimensional logistic posterior, and ULA versus MALA mixing times against dimension — [Biswas et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.09971). The coupled replica-exchange Ising example in Jacob et al. was run on parallel processors — [Jacob et al., arXiv:1708.03625](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.03625)
- The authors' reply points to coupling-from-the-past theory in statistical physics, e.g. a Gumbel limit for rescaled meeting times of a Fortuin–Kasteleyn heat-bath process (Collevecchio et al. 2018) — [JRSSB 2020 with discussion (NSF PAR copy)](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10170901)
- Ascolani & Zanella (2025, rev. 2026) include "an empirical analysis based on coupling techniques" to check theoretical mixing-time bounds for probit data-augmentation Gibbs samplers — [arXiv:2505.14343](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.14343); the companion site describes it as coupling-based TV bounds used to check theory — [unbiased-MCMC companion site](https://pierrejacob.quarto.pub/unbiased-mcmc/articles.html)
- GPU pooled-testing decoding (Aug 2026): the unbiased MCMC framework of Jacob et al. combined with Glynn–Heidelberger-style "slot refilling" for GPU efficiency — [Ueno & Kijima, arXiv:2608.30239](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.30239)
- The companion site's article list (2023–2025 items) has phylogenetics, VAEs and variable-selection applications. It lists no astronomy, physics or ecology applications — [companion site articles](https://pierrejacob.quarto.pub/unbiased-mcmc/articles.html)

#### Wasserstein bounds without couplings, or with CRN only
- Papp & Sherlock, first titled "Bounds on Wasserstein distances between continuous distributions using independent samples" (arXiv:2203.11627, cited under that title by the 2024 review) and retitled "Centered plug-in estimation of Wasserstein distances" (v2, Apr 2025). From independent samples X_{1:n}, X̄_{1:n} ~ μ and Y_{1:n} ~ ν: `U(μ̄_n, μ_n, ν_n) = W2²(μ̄_n, ν_n) − W2²(μ̄_n, μ_n)` and `L = [W2(μ̄_n, ν_n) − W2(μ̄_n, μ_n)]_±` (signed square). U is an approximate upper bound when ν is overdispersed relative to μ, and L an approximate lower bound in general — [Papp & Sherlock, arXiv:2203.11627](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.11627); earlier title as cited in [Atchadé & Jacob 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.06851)
- MCMC use: simulate 2n replicate chains to T ≫ 1 and estimate `L(π̄_n^(T), π_n^(T), π_n^(t)) ≲ W2²(π^(T), π^(t)) ≲ U(π̄_n^(T), π_n^(T), π_n^(t))` when π^(t) is more dispersed than π^(T). The default is n = 1024 replicates. Rates estimated by U "are loose by at most a factor of two". The method also applies to adaptive MCMC. It "can however be vulnerable to issues of pseudo-convergence, since it assumes that the replicate MCMC runs have converged… within the computing budget". Against coupling bounds: "even with the optimal Markovian CRN coupling this bound is relatively loose compared to the estimator U", and "Metropolis accept-reject steps… complicate the design of effective couplings in high dimensions". Appendix E.2 extends Biswas et al. to p-Wasserstein for all p ≥ 1 — [Papp & Sherlock, arXiv:2203.11627](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.11627)
- Sixta, Rosenthal & Brown (arXiv:2309.15735, v5 Sep 2026; a journal DOI 10.1080/15326349.2026.2688991 is listed): with X_0 ~ μ and Y_0 ~ ν independent and π ≪ ν, `W_{d,p}(L(X_N), π) ≤ K_s(π,ν)^{1/r} lim_{M→∞}((1/M)Σ_{i=1}^M d(X_{N,i}, Y_{N,i})^{rs})^{1/(rs)}`, with `K_s(π,ν) = E_{Y_0~ν}[(f_π(Y_0)/f_ν(Y_0))^{s/(s−1)}]^{(s−1)/s}` and the chains run with CRN. The TV special case is ‖L(X_n) − π‖_TV ≤ K_1(π,ν)P(X_n ≠ Y_n), K_1 = ess sup f_π/f_ν. A CLT confidence interval is given (Lemma 3.3). K_s "may become very large as dimension increases" — [Sixta et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.15735); dates and DOI from the [arXiv abstract page](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.15735)
- Biswas & Mackey (JASA 119(548):2947–2958, 2024), "Bounding Wasserstein distance with couplings": the CUB estimator `CUB_{M,N,T} = ((1/(M(T−N))) Σ_{i=1}^M Σ_{n=N+1}^T d(X_{n,i}, Y_{n,i})^p)^{1/p}` bounds W_p between the stationary laws of two *different* kernels (exact versus approximate MCMC) in the limit — [as described by Sixta et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.15735); citation from [Papp & Sherlock](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.11627). Sixta et al. note it "does not tell us precisely when it will happen". Papp & Sherlock report it can be "at least an order of magnitude looser" when unadjusted algorithms are coupled to their Metropolised counterparts — [Papp & Sherlock](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.11627)

#### 2024–2026 coupling-diagnostic follow-ups
- Corenflos & Dau (arXiv:2510.07559; v1 Oct 2025, v3 Jun 2026): "the first general convergence diagnostics for MCMC based on any f-divergence" (KL, χ², Hellinger, TV). A coupling-based "weight harmonization" across 2N interacting chains gives consistent importance weights for dπ/dμ_t. The method is fully online with no lag or warm-up, but is "more conservative than Biswas et al. (2019) for total variation estimation" on all benchmarks. Its χ²-ESS underestimation "worsens with the number of particles". The method needs communication between 2N chains, whereas Biswas et al. is embarrassingly parallel. Code: ImportanceMCMC — [Corenflos & Dau](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.07559)
- Multi-marginal ("grand") couplings of several MH chains (Phan, Flamich, Khisti & Asoodeh; NeurIPS 2026): a shared-randomness Poisson Monte Carlo construction; "reduce meeting times by up to 50% relative to existing baselines" — [NeurIPS 2026 poster page](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2026/poster/149629). The arXiv ID 2605.12807 comes from a search snippet that I did not verify.
- Unbiased estimation of the MCMC asymptotic variance with coupled chains via the Poisson equation (Douc, Jacob, Lee & Vats): `v(P,h) = 2π({h − π(h)}g) − (π(h²) − π(h)²)` and `v̂ = 2(A) − (B)`. It converges at the Monte Carlo rate, whereas batch means converge at T^{−2/3}, but it requires a successful coupling — [Atchadé & Jacob 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.06851)
- Better couplings tighten every coupling diagnostic. Examples: maximal MH couplings (Wang, O'Leary & Jacob 2021); MH kernel coupling characterisation (O'Leary & Wang 2024, *Ann. IHP B*); scalable RWM couplings (Papp & Sherlock, JRSSB 2025, qkae113); crossed-effects Gibbs couplings (Ceriani & Zanella 2024); PDMP couplings (Corenflos, Sutton & Chopin 2025); kinetic Langevin with inexact gradients (Chada et al. 2024); IMH with unbounded weights (Deligiannidis, Jacob, Khribch & Wang 2024–2026, where the CRN coupling of IMH is shown to be maximal and gives polynomial TV bounds) — [companion site articles](https://pierrejacob.quarto.pub/unbiased-mcmc/articles.html); [Deligiannidis et al., arXiv:2411.09514](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.09514); [Papp & Sherlock, arXiv:2211.12585](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.12585)

R: `unbiasedmcmc` (JOA 2020 figures; estimators, meeting times, histograms; last commit Sep 2026), `umcmchandbook` (handbook-chapter figures), `unbiasedpoisson`. ### Inferences
- `telescopic` will have no mature Python reference implementation of L-lag diagnostics to follow. The R packages (`LlagCouplings`, `unbiasedmcmc`) are the de facto references for formulas and output conventions.
- Johnson-style multi-chain coalescence, the CRN Wasserstein bounds of Sixta et al., and Papp & Sherlock's centered plug-in all need either a rejection constant or (approximate) stationarity at T. They are less suitable as default diagnostics for black-box users than the L-lag TV bound. Papp & Sherlock's U/L estimators could still be offered as a coupling-free cross-check from the same replicate chains (n ≈ 1000 replicates needed).
- For astronomy users, the closest validated precedents are Ising/physics-style discrete models and high-dimensional regression. Demonstrations in `telescopic` would themselves be novel applications.

## 3. Practical tuning: k, m (ℓ), L, R; proposal tuning, pilot runs and adaptation; the initial distribution π0; maximum-iteration policies and unmet pairs

### Takeaway
The best-supported default is the Atchadé–Jacob guideline. Draw a pilot batch of meeting times with L = 1, set k to the 99% quantile of τ − L, then set L = k and m = 10k. This typically brings efficiency close to standard MCMC; the "1 + (k + E τ)/(m − k)" loss approximation and published tables support it. R has no rule beyond CLT considerations. Published studies use 100–10,000 replicates, and multimodal cases show that even R = 1000 can mislead. Tuning by pilot runs and frozen settings preserves unbiasedness. Adapting within a run does not, and coupling adaptive samplers is an open problem. π0 should be spread out (overdispersed, covering all modes). Pairs should never be stopped before meeting unless they are explicitly handled as censored or under a Glynn–Heidelberger budget rule.

### Cited Findings

#### Estimator formulas that the tuning acts on (exact indices)
- Unbiased estimators (L = 1): `H_k(X,Y) = h(X_k) + Σ_{t=k+1}^{τ−1} (h(X_t) − h(Y_{t−1}))` and `H_{k:m}(X,Y) = (m−k+1)^{-1} Σ_{l=k}^{m} h(X_l) + Σ_{l=k+1}^{τ−1} min(1, (l−k)/(m−k+1)) (h(X_l) − h(Y_{l−1}))`. The first term is MCMC with burn-in k−1; the second is the bias correction, and it is zero if τ ≤ k+1. Cost: τ−1 calls to P̄ and max(1, m+1−τ) calls to P, i.e. about `2(τ−1) + max(1, m+1−τ)` MCMC-iteration units — [Jacob et al., arXiv:1708.03625](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.03625)
- Lagged, time-averaged estimator (exact weights): `H_{k:ℓ} = (ℓ−k+1)^{-1} Σ_{t=k}^{ℓ} h(X_t) + Σ_{t=k+L}^{τ−1} v_t(k,ℓ,L){h(X_t) − h(Y_{t−L})}`, with `v_t(k,ℓ,L) = (⌊(t−k)/L⌋ − ⌈max(L, t−ℓ)/L⌉ + 1)/(ℓ−k+1)`. Cost: `cost(H_{k:ℓ}) = L + 2(τ − L) + max(0, ℓ − τ)` (one unit for P; two units for P̄ before meeting). The signed measure has `N = max(0, τ − (k+L)) + (ℓ−k+1)` atoms — [Atchadé & Jacob 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.06851)
- The same weights are hard-coded in the R package: `(floor((time−k)/lag) − ceiling(max(lag, time−m)/lag) + 1) * (h(X_t) − h(Y_{t−lag}))`, divided by m−k+1. The cost is `lag + 2*(meetingtime − lag) + max(0, time − meetingtime)` — [unbiasedmcmc R/unbiasedestimator.R](https://github.com/pierrejacob/unbiasedmcmc/blob/master/R/unbiasedestimator.R)
- Biswas et al. define the L-lag estimators as `H_t^(L) = h(X_t) + Σ_{j=1}^{⌈(τ^(L)−L−t)/L⌉} (h(X_{t+jL}) − h(Y_{t+(j−1)L}))` and `H_{k:m}^(L) = (m−k+1)^{-1} Σ_{t=k}^{m} H_t^(L)` — [Biswas et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.09971)

#### Choice of k
- Theory: under a geometric drift condition, `E[BC²_{k:m}] ≤ C_{δ,β} δ_β^k / (m−k+1)²` with δ_β = δ^{1−2β}, so `V[H_{k:m}] ≤ MSE_{k:m} + 2√MSE_{k:m} √(C δ_β^k)/(m−k+1) + C δ_β^k/(m−k+1)²`. "This motivates a choice of k depending on the distribution of the meeting time… we choose k as a large quantile of the meeting times" — [Jacob et al., arXiv:1708.03625](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.03625)
- Published settings: pump-failure Gibbs, k = 7 = 99% quantile of τ from 1000 draws, m = 10k = 70. Bayesian Lasso, k = 99% quantile of 100 meeting times (and another run with "one plus the 90% quantile"), m = 10k, R = 100. German credit Pólya-Gamma, k = 110 and m = 1100, which gave an inefficiency "about 7 times greater" than the underlying Gibbs sampler. Cut distribution, k = 100 and m = 10k after preliminary runs. Baseball, all 1000 pilot τ ≤ 4, k ∈ {1,3,5}, and k = 3, m = 30 gives efficiency ≈ 1 — [Jacob et al., arXiv:1708.03625](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.03625)
- Evidence table (bimodal target, σ_q² = 9, π0 = N(10, 10²), mean τ = 20, 99% quantile 105; h = 1(x > 3); inefficiency / V∞): k=1, m ∈ {k, 10k, 20k} gives 1878.4, 1703.5, 1624.8. k=100 gives 130.6, 2.9, 1.9. k=200 gives 6.5, 1.3, 1.2. The costs for k=200 are 219, 2019, 4019 — [Jacob et al., arXiv:1708.03625](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.03625)
- Counter-evidence for "99% is enough". In a 20-d Gaussian N(0, diag(1², …, 20²)) with reflection-maximal coupled RWM started from N((10, 20, …, 200), I) and m = 10^5, "the mean-squared error (MSE) of unbiased MCMC is prohibitively large until beyond the 0.999-quantile of τ, yet the MSE of the MCMC value might be deemed adequate". Sherlock's heuristic is `var(BC_{k:m} | τ) = O{(τ − k)³/(m − k)²} 1(τ > k+1)` — [Sherlock, JRSSB discussion (NSF PAR copy)](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10170901)

#### Choice of m (ℓ)
- "Choosing m as a multiple of k, such as 5k or 10k, makes intuitive sense when considering that k/m is the proportion of iterations that are simply discarded in the event that τ < k". "For k and m − k both large, the loss of efficiency of the method compared to standard MCMC is approximately 1 + (k + E(τ))/(m − k)" — [Jacob et al., arXiv:1708.03625](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.03625)
- "We… recommend that ℓ is set as a large multiple of k, for example ℓ = 10k". CLT as ℓ → ∞: `√(ℓ−k+1)(H_{k:ℓ} − π(h)) → N(0, v(P,h))`, the same asymptotic variance as MCMC — [Atchadé & Jacob 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.06851)
- A trade-off with parallelism: "one might prefer to keep k and m small, yielding a suboptimal efficiency…, but enabling more independent copies to be generated in a given computing time" — [Jacob et al., arXiv:1708.03625](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.03625)

#### Choice of L for the estimator, and the concrete default guideline
- "Setting L = k leads to a minor increase of cost per estimator compared to L = 1, and thus the efficiency is typically improved, sometimes drastically." **Concrete guideline:** "First generate independent meeting times with lag L = 1 (by lack of a better guess). Then set k as a large (e.g. 99%) quantile of τ − L… Finally, redefine L = k, and set ℓ = 10k." Their Figure 5 shows the relative inefficiency approaching 1 as k grows with ℓ = 10k, "and setting L = k instead of L = 1 is often worthwhile" — [Atchadé & Jacob 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.06851)
- Evidence for lags (Vanetti & Doucet; N(0,1) target, π0 = N(0, 5²), RWM, h(x) = x²; SD of a single estimate): time-averaged lag-1 estimator with (k,m) = (1,10), (10,100), (100,1000) gives 430, 34.2, 0.119. Lagged estimator with L = m − k gives 66.7, 11.9, 0.119 — [Vanetti & Doucet, JRSSB discussion (NSF PAR copy)](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10170901)
- The authors' reply: "Using a value of L that is larger than 1 is always possible and can yield a large improvement in efficiency… We encourage users to implement such lagged estimators". As L increases, "the issue of small meeting times that can occur when two chains start nearby will tend to disappear. Thus users might find lagged estimators with L > 1 to be safer in general" — [JRSSB reply (NSF PAR copy)](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10170901)

#### Number of replicates R, confidence intervals, and parallel budgets
- CI from C independent copies: `[μ̂ + q_{α/2} σ̂/√C, μ̂ + q_{1−α/2} σ̂/√C]`, asymptotically valid as C → ∞ when the variance is finite — [Atchadé & Jacob 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.06851)
- Fixed time budget per processor (Glynn & Heidelberger). Averaging the estimators completed by the deadline is biased: E[H̄_p(t)] = E[H] − E[H 1(C > t)]. The unbiased variant `H̃_p(t) = H̄_p(t)` if N_p(t) > 0, and otherwise the first estimator H_{p1}, which means waiting for one completion. CLT with fixed t and P → ∞: `√P (H̃(P,t) − π(h))/σ̂_1(P,t) → N(0,1)`, where `σ̂_1² = (P−1)^{-1} Σ_p (H̃_p(t) − H̃(P,t))²`. Under geometric tails the expected completion time grows like log P — [Jacob et al., arXiv:1708.03625](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.03625); also explained in [Statisfaction, "Budget-constrained simulations"](https://statisfaction.wordpress.com/2019/02/05/budget-constrained-simulations/)
- "Careful: running unbiased MCMC on M ≫ C machines and retaining the C estimators that are first completed would introduce a bias, since the estimator is not independent of its cost" — [Atchadé & Jacob 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.06851)
- Replicate counts in the literature: N = 10,000 (normal example), N ∈ {1000, 5000, 10,000} (bimodal) and N = 500 (Ising) — [Biswas et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.09971). R = 1000 (bimodal, later + 9000), R = 100 (Lasso), R = 10,000 (cut, baseball) — [Jacob et al., arXiv:1708.03625](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.03625). 100 pairs (GWAS) — [Biswas et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.04798). 100 pairs per lag, or 25 for large trees — [Kelly et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.13328)
- Allocating replicates adaptively across sub-problems: "we can allocate computational resources based on the confidence intervals and the costs associated with each λ… This refinement procedure could be automatized, adaptively producing more estimators for values of λ where confidence intervals are wider" — [Jacob et al., arXiv:1708.03625](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.03625)

#### Proposal and kernel tuning when coupling
- RWM trade-off: larger proposal variance raises the chance of proposing identical values, "however meeting events only occur if proposals are accepted…. This trade-off could lead to a different choice of Σ than the optima known for the marginal chains [Roberts et al., 1997], and deserves further investigation." The experiments use Σ = V/d — [Jacob et al., arXiv:1708.03625](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.03625)
- "We expect the optimal tuning of MCMC kernels to be different in the proposed framework than when used marginally." Parallelism lets users afford costlier kernels, such as more MH steps per component, smaller HMC step sizes and more particles — [Jacob et al., arXiv:1708.03625](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.03625)
- "The HMC tuning parameters associated with the fastest convergence to stationarity might not necessarily be optimal in terms of asymptotic variance" — [Biswas et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.09971)
- The reply on cost: "Selecting the most efficient MCMC kernel available… is obviously recommended. Then, for a fixed MCMC kernel P, reducing the cost amounts to improving the coupling strategy". "'Common random numbers' provide a good starting point… Generating enough contraction to bring the chains close together is the main challenge", with maximal couplings used once the chains are close — [JRSSB reply (NSF PAR copy)](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10170901)
- Reparameterisation helps. Gerber & Lee whiten with Bernstein–von Mises moments (g(x) = A^{-1}(x − μ), AA^T ≈ cov_π) and report average meeting times scaling "(sub)linearly with d" for Metropolis-within-Gibbs on a logistic regression with n = 100d. Chopin found that coupling the residuals rather than the latent z in Albert–Chib probit Gibbs was "20 times faster" — [JRSSB discussion (NSF PAR copy)](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10170901)
- Two-scale couplings: the switching threshold δ between contractive and maximal coupling is chosen "using a grid search on a logarithmic scale" — [Papp & Sherlock, arXiv:2211.12585](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.12585)

#### Pilot runs, and adaptation within versus between replicates
- Pilot-then-freeze precedent (cut distribution): "In preliminary runs… we estimate the first two moments of the cut distribution, and we use them to refine the initial distribution π0 and the proposal covariance matrix Σ" — [Jacob et al., arXiv:1708.03625](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.03625). Lags were chosen by "preliminary runs" — [Biswas et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.04798). "In practice we could choose the number of chains and sequence of lags from a small number of pilot runs" — [Kelly et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.13328)
- Within-run adaptation is unsolved: "A successful coupling of inhomogeneous Markov transitions, e.g. for adaptive MCMC algorithms, remains elusive… it is unclear how to construct a faithful coupling" — [Atchadé & Jacob 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.06851). The reply calls unbiased estimators from adaptive MCMC "a challenging open question" (answering Pompe & Holmes) — [JRSSB reply (NSF PAR copy)](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10170901)
- Between-run heterogeneity: Ryder et al. note that "different pairs of chains on different parallel cores can afford different initial distributions. The resulting estimator remains unbiased." The reply adds that this gives "estimators that are unbiased but not identically distributed, and thus care would be advised for the construction of confidence intervals" — [JRSSB discussion and reply (NSF PAR copy)](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10170901)

#### Initial distribution π0
- "The estimators are unbiased for any choice of π0, including point masses, but this choice has an impact on both the computing cost and the variance." Jacob et al. draw X0 and Y0 independently. "Unfortunate choices of initial distributions can severely affect the performance… This suggests trying more than one choice of initialization, especially in the setting of multimodal targets." Meeting times "can be large even if the chains marginally start at stationarity". For strongly correlated Gibbs, "shorter meeting times are obtained when initializing the chains away from the target" — [Jacob et al., arXiv:1708.03625](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.03625)
- Meeting-time tails depend on π0 through π0(V): under geometric drift with level-set small sets and a one-step meeting probability ≥ ε on C×C, `P(τ > n) ≤ C′ π0(V) κ^n` with π0(V) = ∫V dπ0 — [Jacob et al., arXiv:1708.03625](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.03625)
- The finite-moment theorem in the review assumes `dπ0/dπ ≤ M`, which "rules out the choice of π0 as a Dirac mass" for continuous π, "but it allows π0 to be the Uniform distribution on any ball with positive radius" — [Atchadé & Jacob 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.06851)
- Spread-out initialisation proposals: Ryder et al. draw Z ~ Bernoulli(½) and set (X0, Y0) ~ (p, p′) or (p′, p), so both marginals are ½(p + p′) but the chains start in different regions. The reply suggests making π0 a mixture and maximising the probability that X0 and Y0 come from different components — [JRSSB discussion and reply (NSF PAR copy)](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10170901). Phylogenetics initialised each chain from short prior runs because random trees are nearly maximally far apart in SPR distance — [Kelly et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.13328). GWAS chains were initialised "independently from the prior" — [Biswas et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.04798). "The prior is often a suitable initialization, because it tends to be less concentrated than the (target) posterior" — [Papp & Sherlock, arXiv:2203.11627](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.11627)
- If an exact draw from π is affordable once (accept–reject, linchpin variables), starting standard MCMC there makes it "trivially unbiased" with no burn-in — [Vats & Jones, JRSSB discussion (NSF PAR copy)](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10170901)

#### Maximum-iteration policies, truncation and unmet pairs
- "Unreliable estimators would also result from stopping the chains before their meeting time" — [Jacob et al., arXiv:1708.03625](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.03625)
- The R package's `max_iterations` defaults to `Inf`. If it is hit, `meetingtime` is returned as `Inf` with no warning, and the returned "unbiased" estimate silently omits the rest of the bias correction — [unbiasedmcmc R/unbiasedestimator.R](https://github.com/pierrejacob/unbiasedmcmc/blob/master/R/unbiasedestimator.R)
- The Biswas et al. scripts use caps from 1000 to 10^7 (and `Inf` for the bimodal study). Unmet pairs get τ = ∞, which makes the averaged TV bound infinite — [LlagCouplings repository](https://github.com/niloyb/LlagCouplings)
- Truncated scaling studies report medians: "we have stopped the runs after 500,000 iterations; the median is robust to this truncation, but not the average" — [Jacob et al., arXiv:1708.03625](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.03625)
- Random truncation (Glynn & Rhee; Agapiou et al.) is the alternative to waiting for meetings, using single-term `G = a_ξ/p_ξ` or coupled-sum `H = a_0 + Σ_{k=1}^{ξ} a_k/P(ξ ≥ k)` estimators. It trades cost against variance through the truncation law. Its construction "can require hard-to-determine quantities, such as the contraction rate" — [Atchadé & Jacob 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.06851); [JRSSB reply (NSF PAR copy)](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10170901)

### Inferences
- **Implement the exact lag-L weights, and test them.** The lagged time-averaged estimator printed in the JRSSB reply reads, in my PDF text extraction, `H^(L)_{k:m} = (m−k+1)^{-1} Σ_{l=k}^{m} h(X_l) + Σ_{l=k+L}^{τ−1} min(1, ⟨(l−k)/L⟩/(m−k+1)) {h(X_l) − h(Y_{l−L})}`. The rounding bracket ⟨·⟩ was lost in extraction — [JRSSB reply (NSF PAR copy)](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10170901). I checked candidate weightings numerically against direct averaging of H_t^(L) over t = k, …, m, using thousands of random (k, m, L, τ) configurations with synthetic faithful chains (my own script, not from any source):
  - The review's weights v_t(k,ℓ,L) (eq. 2.10), also used in the `unbiasedmcmc` code, match to about 1e−14.
  - The simplified form `min(1, ⌊(l−k)/L⌋/(m−k+1))` matches only when τ ≤ m + L + 1, which is the common regime m ≫ τ but not in general.
  - A ceiling-based form does not match for L > 1.
  `telescopic` should implement eq. (2.10) and unit-test it against brute-force averaging of H_t. Algebraically, (2.10) with L = 1 reduces to the familiar `min(1, (l−k)/(m−k+1))`, because (t − k − max(1, t − ℓ) + 1) = min(t − k, ℓ − k + 1).
- **Default pipeline for `telescopic`** (my synthesis of the guideline and the evidence):
  1. Pilot: R₀ ≈ 100–200 pairs with L = 1 and a generous cap; record censoring.
  2. Set k = ⌈q̂_{0.99}(τ − L)⌉, then L = k and m = 10k.
  3. Main run: R pairs. Report the TV-bound curve from the main run's lag and the estimated t̂_mix(0.25) and t̂_mix(0.01).
  4. Offer a "conservative" mode with k at the 99.9% quantile (Sherlock's example) and a "parallel-cheap" mode with smaller k and m (Jacob et al.'s trade-off).
- **Pilot replicates and unbiasedness.** If tuning is done on pilot replicates that are then discarded, the final replicates stay i.i.d. and the standard CLT confidence interval applies. Letting the tuning of replicate r depend on replicates 1, …, r−1 keeps each replicate conditionally unbiased given its tuning, hence the average unbiased (tower property). However, the replicates are then neither independent nor identically distributed, so the i.i.d. CLT does not directly apply, in line with the reply's warning on heterogeneous π0. Data-dependent stopping of R (stop when the CI is narrow) is a form of random sample size that can bias the average, by analogy with the Glynn–Heidelberger budget results. Safer default: freeze the tuning after the pilot and fix R or the time budget in advance.
- **Unmet pairs.** Treat pairs hitting the cap as right-censored observations of τ. Never silently include them in point estimates, and report the censored fraction prominently. For the TV bound, a censored pair at T_max contributes at least ⌈(T_max + 1 − L − t)/L⌉ at time t, so its observed contribution is a lower bound on its true one. The estimated bound with censored pairs is therefore anti-conservative unless they are counted as ∞, as the reference code does. For estimators, either (a) keep running (default, since the cap only protects against hangs) or (b) under an explicit time budget, apply the Glynn–Heidelberger rule per worker: average completed replicates, discard in-flight ones, and wait for at least one per worker.
- The R package's silent `Inf` meeting time is a usability hazard that `telescopic` should not copy. Raise a warning or error and attach the censoring status to each replicate.

### Gaps
- No source gives a validated rule for R, or for the pilot size needed to estimate a 99% quantile of τ reliably. Meeting-time distributions with rare long excursions (multimodality) defeat any fixed pilot size.
- No source formally analyses adaptation *between* replicates, such as proposal covariance learned from earlier replicates. The reasoning above is my own.
- No source studies optimal proposal scaling for coupled chains, i.e. scaling for efficiency of unbiased estimators rather than of the marginal chain. Jacob et al. call it an open question.

## 4. Failure modes and warning signs: multimodality, heavy-tailed meeting times and infinite variance, high dimension and poor proposal overlap, numerical problems, and documented misleading cases

### Takeaway
Unbiasedness is an *expectation* statement. Finite-R results can be badly wrong when the meeting-time distribution has rare, huge values. This happens when π0 sits in one mode of a multimodal target (fast meetings in one mode, rare cross-mode excursions), when meeting times have polynomial or heavy tails (pseudo-marginal, strongly correlated Gibbs, latent-variable models), and when couplings fail to contract in high dimension (meeting times then grow exponentially in d). Documented misleading results include a 95% CI of [0.799, 0.816] for a quantity whose true value is 0.421 (R = 1000), and TV-bound estimates that were over-confident with N = 1000 pairs. The warning signs are long right tails or outliers in meeting times, a large empirical variance or individual outlying estimates, plateaus in the TV bound, a large gap between the 80% and 99% quantiles, pairs that fail to meet, and very similar states that do not meet. Coupling can expose multimodality (as large or unmet τ) or hide it (when π0 never reaches the other modes within the observed runs).

### Cited Findings

#### Multimodal targets: what meeting times do
- Bimodal π = ½N(−4,1) + ½N(4,1) with RWM, three regimes. (1) σ_q² = 9, π0 = N(10, 10²): mean τ 20, 99% quantile 105, accurate results. (2) σ_q² = 1, same π0: mean τ 769, 99% quantile 9186; "when the chains start in different modes, the meeting times are often dramatically larger"; accurate only with k = 20,000 and m = 30,000 (CI [0.397, 0.430] for truth ≈ 0.421). (3) σ_q² = 1, π0 = N(10, 1): mean τ 9 and 99% quantile 35. The 95% CI from R = 1000 with k = 50, m = 500 is **[0.799, 0.816], "far from the true value of 0.421"**. 9000 more estimators gave [−0.353, 1.595], because a few runs had one chain jump to the left mode before meeting. "Upon noticing a large empirical variance one can then decide to use larger values of k and m… poor performance of the underlying Markov chains combined with ill-chosen initializations can still produce misleading results for any finite R" — [Jacob et al., arXiv:1708.03625](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.03625)
- The corresponding TV-bound failure (N = 1000, L = 1, "can be misleading") and the plateau seen with L = 18,000, "reflects how the chains first visit one of the modes, and then both" — [Biswas et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.09971)
- "Slow mixing between modes… manifests as plateaus in the bound". "The coupling diagnostic will only be as reliable as the distribution of the meeting times." Their advice: initialise far apart and increase the lag "to avoid the illusion of convergence from slow mixing components" — [Kelly et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.13328)
- Couplings as a warning: an undetected bad π0 "occurs… because the coupling of the chains occurs long before the chain reaches stationarity"; but "E[τ] is large, and this is a feature, since it warns the practitioner that their kernel is ill fitted to the target density" (Ryder et al.). The authors agree that "large meeting times can sometimes indicate convergence issues… and can indeed be seen as a feature rather than a bug" — [JRSSB discussion and reply (NSF PAR copy)](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10170901)
- Coupling is not convergence: "It is important to keep in mind that coalescence of the coupled chains and convergence to equilibrium are different." Engineering early coupling (identical initial conditions, low-acceptance kernels) "might be a dangerous strategy" — [Wilkinson, JRSSB discussion (NSF PAR copy)](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10170901)
- "Coupling pairs of Markov chains does not improve their marginal mixing properties… poor mixing of the underlying chains can lead to poor performance of the resulting estimator" — [Jacob et al., arXiv:1708.03625](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.03625)
- Separating bad coupling from bad mixing: when Lasso meeting times peaked near λ = 10², the authors ran the plain Gibbs sampler and computed CODA ESS. A "drastic loss of efficiency around λ = 10²" showed that the sampler, not the coupling, was at fault — [Jacob et al., arXiv:1708.03625](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.03625)

#### Heavy-tailed meeting times and infinite variance
- Moment conditions (review, from Douc et al. 2023). Assumption 1: for chains started independently from π and evolved by P̄, `E[τ^κ] < ∞` for some κ ≥ 1. With κ > 1, h ∈ L^m(π), m > κ/(κ−1) and dπ0/dπ ≤ M, the estimator is unbiased and has a finite p-th moment for `1/p > 1/m + 1/κ`. Finite variance therefore needs `κ > 2` and `m > 2κ/(κ − 2)`; geometric tails allow any h ∈ L^{2+η} — [Atchadé & Jacob 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.06851)
- Polynomial tails (pseudo-marginal and other polynomially ergodic chains) are allowed if `P(τ > n) ≤ K n^{−κ}` with `κ > 2(2η^{−1} + 1)` (η from E|h|^{2+η} moments). A polynomial drift condition with exponent α gives `P(τ ≥ n) ≤ K n^{−κ}`, κ = 1/(1−α). With η = 1 this needs κ > 6, i.e. α > 5/6 — [Middleton, Deligiannidis, Doucet & Jacob 2020, arXiv:1807.08691](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.08691)
- Observed heavy tails, pseudo-marginal: "the smaller difference between the 80th percentiles, compared to the difference between the 99th percentiles, reflects a heavier tail of the distribution of the meeting time in the case of PMMH". With bootstrap particle filters (N = 4096) on 400 processors, meeting times were "larger, and heavier tailed" with a maximum of 9371. Meeting times shrink as the number of particles grows, and inefficiency approaches that of coupled exact MH — [Middleton et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.08691)
- Observed heavy tails, Gibbs on an AR(1) latent field (T = 200, α = 0.99): "the mean coupling time is around 2.6 × 10³, with a median of around 1 × 10³. The distribution is long tailed, with a maximum observed coupling time over 1000 replicates of 67000." Two of six replicates failed to coalesce within 500 iterations. "Choosing k to be a very high quantile of this distribution will therefore be computationally demanding" — [Wilkinson, JRSSB discussion (NSF PAR copy)](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10170901)
- In Sherlock's 20-d example the error is dominated by "the most extreme τ-values" ("a few very influential simulations had extremely large values for τ"). His Rao–Blackwellisation for MH integrates over coupling events, cutting the MSE "an order of magnitude at the 0.99-quantile and a factor of 30 at k = 10^4.25, at a cost of less than twice that of unbiased MCMC" — [Sherlock, JRSSB discussion (NSF PAR copy)](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10170901)
- Wall-clock: with one estimator per processor the run takes as long as the slowest pair. Under geometric tails the "average maximum cost… behaves as log(C)" — [Atchadé & Jacob 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.06851). For coupled conditional backward-sampling particle filters, E[max of P coupling times] = O(T + log P), giving minimal wall time O(T² + T log P) — [Lee, Singh & Vihola, JRSSB discussion (NSF PAR copy)](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10170901)

#### High dimension and poor proposal overlap
- RWM with *maximal* coupling of proposals: "the plot indicates an exponential increase of the average meeting times with the dimension" (d ≤ 5 shown), from both target and offset initialisations. With *reflection-maximal* coupling the increase "appears closer to linear" (d ≤ 50). Gibbs with strong correlations scales poorly (runs stopped at 500,000), while weak correlations scale sub-linearly — [Jacob et al., arXiv:1708.03625](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.03625)
- Why: with h = ℓ d^{−1/2}, `P(X_{t+1} = Y_{t+1} | X_t, Y_t) ≤ 2Φ(−‖X_t − Y_t‖/(2h)) ≤ 2 exp(−d‖X_t − Y_t‖²/(8ℓ²))`. Independent starts are O(d) apart in squared distance, but coalescence needs O(d^{−1}). "If the coupling cannot contract the chains to within, say, O(1) squared distance, then one can expect meeting times to grow exponentially with the dimension." Imbalanced accept/reject events between the chains cause Θ(1) expansions, so scalable couplings must synchronise acceptance. GCRN (gradient-based CRN) is "provably optimally contractive"; the reflection coupling has a shortcoming, which GCRefl mitigates. Two-scale schemes are advocated — [Papp & Sherlock, arXiv:2211.12585](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.12585) (JRSSB 2025, qkae113)
- Latent-variable and missing-data models: with high-dimensional latent variables, "the expected meeting time E[τ] will be extremely large" (Gu). Paulin asks whether couplings with good dimension dependence exist for MALA, HMC and Gibbs — [JRSSB discussion (NSF PAR copy)](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10170901)
- HMC: "the coupling HMC kernel of Heng and Jacob (2019) only couples quickly if the problem is sufficiently well conditioned that HMC converges rapidly with a relatively small number of integration steps per proposal" — [Margossian et al., nested R̂, arXiv:2110.13017](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.13017)
- Non-overlapping proposals in practice: for non-identifiable parameters (no lateral transfer in the data), samples "get arbitrarily small but may fail to meet… because proposal distributions in the two states do not overlap". Pairs in "very similar states but failing to meet" were traced to correlated components (topology and catastrophes) that needed a joint update. Proposals that rescale several parameters by a common factor are hard to couple — [Kelly et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.13328)

#### Numerical and computational problems
- Maximal coupling by rejection (Thorisson/Johnson γ-coupling): the number of draws from ν has mean 1 and variance `2μ(φ)/{1 − μ(φ)}`. With φ = min{1, w} this variance → ∞ as TV(μ,ν) → 0. Using φ = min{C, w} with C < 1 bounds it by 2C/(1−C) at the price of a sub-maximal coupling — [Gerber & Lee, JRSSB discussion (NSF PAR copy)](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10170901). The review's Algorithm 2 carries this η ∈ (0,1] parameter — [Atchadé & Jacob 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.06851)
- Pure CRN or synchronous couplings contract but never produce exact equality in continuous spaces. CRN-only chains "may come arbitrarily close but will never exactly meet" — [Biswas et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.04798). "Exact simulation of d(X_n, Y_n) = 0 was not realistically attainable" — [Sixta et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.15735). HMC couplings therefore mix in a RWM kernel with a maximal coupling to allow exact meetings. Biswas et al. use mixture weight γ = 0.05 and σ_MH = 0.001 — [Biswas et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.09971). Switching between the two kernels based on the current distance would be illegal ("it would not be legal to employ P̄1 when the chains are distant and P̄2 when they are close") — [Atchadé & Jacob 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.06851)
- Implementation conventions in reference code:
  - Meetings are flagged by the coupling event (`identical = proposal_identical && accept1 && accept2`), not by comparing floats afterwards.
  - On a common proposal the second chain reuses the first chain's proposal and its log-density.
  - Maximal-coupling acceptance is computed in log space (`dp(x) + log(U) < dq(x)`).
  - Non-finite log-densities are rejected.
  - After meeting, the code sets `state2 <- state1` and runs only the single kernel, which enforces faithfulness by construction.
  - Source: [unbiasedmcmc R/get_mh_kernels.R](https://github.com/pierrejacob/unbiasedmcmc/blob/master/R/get_mh_kernels.R), [R/get_max_coupling.R](https://github.com/pierrejacob/unbiasedmcmc/blob/master/R/get_max_coupling.R), [R/unbiasedestimator.R](https://github.com/pierrejacob/unbiasedmcmc/blob/master/R/unbiasedestimator.R). The handbook's R code also returns `nextxequalsnexty = sameprop && xaccept && yaccept` — [Atchadé & Jacob 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.06851)
- Out-of-range estimates: estimators "can take negative values even if the range of the test function contains only non-negative values". The proportion of negative weights can be reduced by increasing k, "but we cannot make it zero". There is no general fix: no algorithm turns unbiased estimators of a non-negative quantity into non-negative unbiased ones (Jacob & Thiery 2015) — [Jacob et al., arXiv:1708.03625](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.03625); [Atchadé & Jacob 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.06851)

#### Documented cases where results misled or were wrong
- The bimodal CI [0.799, 0.816] versus truth 0.421 with R = 1000 — [Jacob et al., arXiv:1708.03625](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.03625)
- TV bounds from N = 1000 at L = 1 on a bimodal target ("can be misleading") — [Biswas et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.09971)
- A code bug in Biswas et al.'s Fig. 5 (ULA versus MALA mixing-time bounds against dimension). The step size meant to be h ∝ d^{−1/6} was implemented as ε = h² ∝ d^{−1/6}, "leading to a decaying acceptance rate for MALA as dimension grew". The corrected study is based on 50 meetings per configuration — [LlagCouplings fig5_erratum.tex](https://github.com/niloyb/LlagCouplings/blob/master/ula_versus_mala/fig5_erratum.tex)
- 20-d Gaussian: MSE prohibitive until k exceeds the 0.999 quantile — [Sherlock, JRSSB discussion (NSF PAR copy)](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10170901)
- The field-standard ASDSF diagnostic declaring convergence while coupling bounds show slow convergence of other model components (16 taxa) — [Kelly et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.13328)

### Inferences
- **Proposed warnings for `telescopic`** (heuristics from the documented cases; thresholds need calibration):
  - (a) Any censored pair (unmet at the cap).
  - (b) Heavy tail in τ: q̂_{0.99}/q̂_{0.8} or max τ / median τ large (≳ 10, as in Wilkinson's AR(1) example). Also compute a Hill-type tail-index estimate; a value ≲ 2 means Theorem 2.1's finite-variance condition κ > 2 may fail.
  - (c) Estimator outliers: a single replicate contributing a large share (for example > 25%) of the sample variance, or the estimate moving by more than a few SE when the largest-τ replicates are removed.
  - (d) A non-trivial fraction (for example > 1–5%) of replicates with τ − L > k, i.e. a non-zero bias correction.
  - (e) TV-bound curve: a plateau, or a value > 1 at t = 0 (increase L).
  - (f) Disagreement between the pilot and main-run meeting-time distributions, or between runs from two different π0 (for example the default π0 and a wider one), in the spirit of Ryder et al.'s different-initialisation check.
  - (g) Very similar but unmet states (distance below a small tolerance for many iterations), which points to coupling design problems such as CRN-only couplings or non-overlapping proposals.
  - (h) Relative inefficiency ≫ 1 versus an MCMC baseline.
- Make (b)–(c) prominent: in every documented failure the *point estimate and its SE looked fine* until rare long meetings were observed. The SE from R replicates cannot certify finite variance.
- Use thresholds and log-space for the maximal-coupling rejection loop, with a sub-maximal η < 1 option, to avoid rare runaway loops when proposals nearly coincide.

### Gaps
- No source gives calibrated thresholds for any warning statistic (tail index, quantile ratios, variance shares). The proposals above are heuristics.
- Beyond the code conventions, I found no literature on floating-point issues. Examples: chains that should be identical diverging because of non-deterministic operation order, GPU non-determinism, or equality tests on derived states. A targeted search returned nothing.
- Exactly how multimodality affects the *variance* of the bound estimator, as opposed to the bound itself, is described only qualitatively.

## 5. Coupling diagnostics versus standard diagnostics (rank-normalised R̂ and ESS, nested R̂, integrated autocorrelation time as in emcee)

### Takeaway
They answer different questions. Coupling bounds give a non-asymptotic upper bound, in expectation, on the TV or W1 distance to π at a finite iteration t, from the chosen π0 and the chosen coupling. They need no stationarity assumption, but they need a working coupling, they are loose, and their Monte Carlo estimate can be over-confident. R̂, ESS and IAT assume (approximate) stationarity and target the variance of time averages (asymptotic efficiency). They cannot certify burn-in bias, although multiple dispersed chains do help reveal multimodality. Vehtari et al. recommend R̂ < 1.01, at least 4 chains and bulk/tail-ESS > 400. emcee recommends chains longer than about 50τ. Nested R̂ adapts R̂ to the many-short-chains regime, which is closest to unbiased MCMC.

### Cited Findings
- **Rank-normalised R̂ and ESS (Vehtari, Gelman, Simpson, Carpenter & Bürkner, *Bayesian Analysis* 2021).**
  - Use the sample only if R̂ < 1.01, "much tighter than the one recommended by Gelman and Rubin (1992)". R̂ "can dip below 1.1 well before convergence in some examples".
  - Run "at least four chains by default. Multiple chains are more likely to reveal multimodality".
  - Require rank-normalised ESS > 400 (each of the split chains with average ESS ≥ 50).
  - "For multimodal distributions with well-separated modes, the split-R̂ adjustment leads to an ESS estimate that is close to the number of distinct modes… ESS can be drastically overestimated if computed from a single chain."
  - "A small value of R̂ is not enough to ensure that an MCMC sample is useful in practice".
  - Traditional R̂ fails "when the chain has a heavy tail". Tail-ESS covers extreme quantiles.
  - Massive parallelisation (≥ 1000 chains) is not considered: "further research is needed (see… Jacob et al. 2017)".
  - Sources: [Vehtari et al., arXiv:1903.08008](https://arxiv.org/abs/1903.08008)
- **Nested R̂ (Margossian, Hoffman, Sountsov, Riou-Durand, Vehtari & Gelman, *Bayesian Analysis* 2024)**, for many short chains organised into superchains with a common initialisation.
  - Check R̂_ν ≤ 1 + ε. With a single sampling iteration per chain (N = 1) the threshold is `R̂_ν ≤ √(1 + 1/M + τ)`. Here M is the number of subchains per superchain, and τ is a user-chosen tolerance on the scaled nonstationary variance ("the choice of τ… depends on the problem"), not an autocorrelation time.
  - Recommend K between 8 and 256 initialisations when running KM = 2048 chains.
  - On unbiased MCMC: "the coupling time replaces the traditional warmup phase", but "at present it is not always possible to find a kernel that couples quickly".
  - Sources: [Margossian et al., arXiv:2110.13017](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.13017)
- **Integrated autocorrelation time (emcee).**
  - `τ_f = Σ_{τ=−∞}^{∞} ρ_f(τ)`, estimated with Sokal's automated window: the smallest M with M ≥ C τ̂_f(M), C ≈ 5.
  - "Chains longer than about 50τ are often sufficient". Do not trust τ̂ unless you have more than about F×τ samples with F ≥ 50.
  - Autocorrelation is now computed per walker and then averaged; the older mean-chain method "can give dangerously over-confident estimates". Both estimators "give outrageous results for the short chains".
  - "It isn't formally possible to guarantee convergence for any but the simplest models."
  - Source: [emcee autocorrelation tutorial](https://emcee.readthedocs.io/en/stable/tutorials/autocorr/)
- **Different targets.** ESS/R̂-type criteria "rely on the asymptotic behavior of the chains as time goes to infinity, and thus effectively ignore the non-asymptotic 'burn-in' bias… acceptable in situations where we have solid a priori estimates of the burn-in period" — [Biswas et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.04798). Autocorrelation and ESS work concerns asymptotic variance, while the coupling bounds target non-asymptotic bias; Jacob calls the two "largely unrelated" — [Statisfaction 2019](https://statisfaction.wordpress.com/2019/06/05/estimating-convergence-of-markov-chains/)
- **Monte Carlo error.** For unbiased MCMC the asymptotic variance v(P,h) can itself be estimated without bias from coupled chains, converging at the Monte Carlo rate, whereas batch means converges at T^{−2/3} — [Atchadé & Jacob 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.06851)
- **Gelman–Rubin-style diagnostics** target "the agreement in terms of a specific functional, rather than a more fundamental property of the chain itself" — [Corenflos & Dau 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.07559)
- **Head-to-head comparisons.** Coupling TV bounds versus ASDSF in phylogenetics (sometimes earlier, sometimes more stringent): [Kelly et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.13328). The coupling bound versus the replicate-based centered W2 estimator, where the coupling bound is looser and its sharpness "highly dependent on the coupling used" (CRN versus reflection-maximal): [Papp & Sherlock, arXiv:2203.11627](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.11627)
- **Complementary use.** Standard CODA ESS on long plain runs separated "poor mixing" from "poor coupling" when meeting times were large — [Jacob et al., arXiv:1708.03625](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.03625)

### Inferences
- **What each guarantees, summarised for users:**
  - *L-lag TV/W1 bound:* a rigorous upper bound in expectation on distance-to-target at iteration t, for π0 and the coupling used. No stationarity assumption, but the estimate is Monte Carlo and fails silently if rare long meetings are unobserved.
  - *Unbiased estimator ± CLT CI:* exact unbiasedness and asymptotically valid CIs, if the variance is finite. Finite variance cannot be checked from the output.
  - *R̂/ESS/IAT:* heuristic consistency checks plus Monte Carlo standard errors under approximate stationarity. They cannot bound burn-in bias, and they can be fooled by modes no chain visits. The same is true of coupling diagnostics with a mode-concentrated π0.
- `telescopic` should report both families. Coupling diagnostics come for free from the meeting times. Classical summaries can be computed on the X-chains after k (for example a rank-normalised R̂ across replicates' post-k segments, or IAT on the long X-chains when m ≫ k), giving users the familiar emcee-style IAT and R̂ numbers. These may disagree with the coupling bound, and disagreement is itself a warning.
- emcee users expect an "ensemble" sampler. Coupling an affine-invariant ensemble move is not covered by any source I found, so IAT-style guidance cannot transfer directly to coupled ensemble samplers.

### Gaps
- I found no systematic empirical study comparing coupling bounds with rank-normalised R̂, ESS and IAT on common benchmarks, in particular rates of false "converged" verdicts.
- I found no source on coupling affine-invariant ensemble samplers (emcee's default move) for unbiased estimation or diagnostics.

## 6. Reporting conventions in papers and software: estimates, standard errors and CIs, signed-measure histograms, meeting-time distributions, efficiency comparisons, and useful visualisations

### Takeaway
Established conventions:
- Point estimates are averages of R independent replicates with CLT-based 95% CIs.
- Histograms use per-bin unbiased estimates with ±2 SE boxes, so bins can go negative.
- Meeting times are shown as histograms (of τ or τ − L), as quantiles (80%, 90%, 99%), as averages or medians against dimension or problem size, and as 1 − ECDF on a log scale to check tails.
- TV and W1 bounds are plotted against iteration (often log axes) for several lags.
- Efficiency is reported as inefficiency = E[cost] × Var(H), divided by the MCMC asymptotic variance V∞, with cost counted in kernel-call units (coupled kernel = 2).
- The authors ask that comparisons with "one long run" state the processors, budget, precision, k, m and burn-in.

### Cited Findings
- **CIs.** CIs come from the CLT over replicates. Histograms "are produced by averaging unbiased estimators of expectations of indicator functions, corresponding to consecutive intervals. Confidence intervals at level 95%… are represented as grey boxes, with vertical bars showing the point estimates" — [Jacob et al., arXiv:1708.03625](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.03625). The R package's `histogram_c_chains` returns per-bin proportions and SEs (sd/√R). `plot_histogram` draws rectangles at density ± 2 SE (proportion/width) with point-estimate segments — [unbiasedmcmc R/histogram_c_chains.R](https://github.com/pierrejacob/unbiasedmcmc/blob/master/R/histogram_c_chains.R)
- **Signed measure.** `π̂ = (m−k+1)^{-1} Σ_{l=k}^{m} δ_{X_l} + Σ_{l=k+1}^{τ−1} min(1, (l−k)/(m−k+1))(δ_{X_l} − δ_{Y_{l−1}})` (L = 1). The weights sum to 1 and some are negative. `F̂^R(s) = Σ_l ω_l 1(Z_l ≤ s)` converges uniformly to F (Proposition 3.2) but is not monotone. Quantiles are taken as any Z_l where the cumulative weight crosses q. CIs for quantiles ("perhaps by bootstrapping") are left open, as are projection onto probability measures and isotonic regression — [Jacob et al., arXiv:1708.03625](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.03625). General lag version (eq. 2.12) with weights v_t(k,ℓ,L). The review's Figure 4 shows KDEs of the weighted atoms split into "MCMC + positive bias correction + negative bias correction". Sub-sampling picks an index I uniformly and weights by Nω_I (unbiased, but not a perfect sample) — [Atchadé & Jacob 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.06851)
- **Efficiency.** `inefficiency = E[cost] × V[H_{k:m}]`, reported divided by V∞. V∞ was estimated with CODA spectral methods from long runs (10^6 iterations, 10^4 burn-in; or 5×10^5 and 10^3). Tables of cost, variance and inefficiency/V∞ over a (k, m) grid are standard. "Efficiency" = 1/(cost × variance): pump example 0.94 (unbiased, k = 7, m = 70) versus 1.06 (regeneration) versus 1.08 (plain Gibbs) — [Jacob et al., arXiv:1708.03625](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.03625). The review plots inefficiency relative to MCMC against mean cost per estimator for several (k, L) configurations (Fig. 5) — [Atchadé & Jacob 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.06851)
- **Cost units.** The package returns `mcmcestimator`, `correction`, `uestimator`, `meetingtime`, `iteration`, `elapsedtime` and `cost` ("computing cost in terms of calls to Markov kernels (counting coupled kernel as twice the cost)") — [unbiasedmcmc R/unbiasedestimator.R](https://github.com/pierrejacob/unbiasedmcmc/blob/master/R/unbiasedestimator.R). A discussant asked why "average meeting time" is used as a speed measure when "the word 'time' makes me relate this term to the actual computation time" — [Chai, JRSSB discussion (NSF PAR copy)](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10170901)
- **Meeting-time reporting:**
  - Histograms of τ − L (10^4 copies): [Atchadé & Jacob 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.06851).
  - Average or median meeting time against dimension (median when runs are truncated): [Jacob et al., arXiv:1708.03625](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.03625).
  - 80th and 99th percentiles against data size T, and meeting times plotted against wall-clock duration per processor: [Middleton et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.08691).
  - 1 − ECDF on a log scale: [Kelly et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.13328).
  - log10(1 + MSE) against log10 k with τ_{0.99}, τ_{0.999} and max τ marked: [Sherlock, JRSSB discussion (NSF PAR copy)](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10170901).
- **Bounds plots.** TV and W1 bounds against iteration for several lags; exact distances overlaid when known; log axes; mixing-time bound against dimension — [Biswas et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.09971); [Atchadé & Jacob 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.06851)
- **Parallel reporting.** Pairs of (precision, expected completion time) over budgets T and processor counts P (Table 12) illustrate when doubling P doubles precision at fixed run time and when it halves run time. "Comparisons between unbiased MCMC estimators and 'one long run'… would be most informative with adequate amounts of context, including the number of processors, budget constraints, the desired precision, the choice of k and m… and the choice of burn-in for the MCMC algorithm" — [JRSSB reply (NSF PAR copy)](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10170901)
- **Robust aggregation.** Users can "readily replace empirical averages by more robust estimators of expectations": trimmed means (Nguyen et al. 2022), median-of-means, or estimators based on self-normalised sums — [Atchadé & Jacob 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.06851)
- **Interval terminology.** A discussant asked why confidence rather than credible intervals are used. The authors replied that the method is "not specifically Bayesian": the intervals are frequentist Monte Carlo intervals for π(h) — [JRSSB discussion and reply (NSF PAR copy)](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10170901)

### Inferences
- **Default summary for `telescopic`:**
  - Estimate, SE, 95% CI and R.
  - k, m, L, π0 description and coupling type.
  - Meeting-time summary: median, q90, q99, max, number censored.
  - Mean cost per replicate (kernel-call units and wall-clock).
  - Fraction of replicates with a non-zero bias correction.
  - Relative inefficiency versus an IAT-based MCMC baseline when available.
  - Warning flags (section 4).
- **Default plots:** (1) histogram and log-scale 1 − ECDF of τ − L; (2) TV bound against iteration (log-y) with the chosen L and the k marker; (3) per-parameter marginal histograms with ±2 SE boxes (negative bins allowed and explained); (4) optionally, the trace of distance between the chains for a few pairs, which shows contraction and helps diagnose coupling failure.
- The signed measure needs a clear user-facing explanation: negative weights and estimates outside the support are expected and do not indicate a bug, and quantiles from a non-monotone CDF are only consistent. Offer isotonic or projected CDFs for display only, with unbiased bin estimates kept for inference.

### Gaps
- I found no community standard for reporting unbiased MCMC results in applied-science journals. The conventions above come from methodological papers and their software.
- Valid CIs for quantiles from signed measures remain open, as Jacob et al. state.

## 7. Critiques and open problems: the JRSSB 2020 discussion, the reply, and later commentary

### Takeaway
The read paper (11 Dec 2019; JRSSB 82(3):543–600) drew 19 discussion contributions: 5 at the meeting and 14 in writing. It was broadly praised. The central critiques were:
- Burn-in is corrected rather than eliminated, so it is repeated per pair. This limits parallel speed-up (Amdahl's law).
- Coalescence is not convergence.
- Heavy-tailed meeting times make high quantiles of τ costly, and the bias-correction noise is dominated by extreme τ.
- Coupling designs scale poorly in high dimension and in latent-variable or correlated Gibbs settings.
- Unbiasedness is not the ultimate goal; there is no free lunch.
- Adaptive MCMC cannot currently be coupled.
- Theory on meeting times is thin.

Proposed fixes were lagged estimators (adopted as standard), control variates, Rao–Blackwellisation, antithetic or correlated pairs, alternative initialisation schemes, reparameterisation and controlled-variance maximal couplings. Later commentary (2022–2026) frames unbiased MCMC as a "sensible balance" between perfect sampling and MCMC. Its open questions are that successful couplings are not guaranteed to exist, that adaptive coupling is unsolved, and that quantile inference and non-negativity have no general fix.

### Cited Findings
- **Venue.** "Read before The Royal Statistical Society… on Wednesday, December 11th, 2019, Professor A. Doucet in the Chair". JRSSB 82, Part 3, pp. 543–600 — [JRSSB 2020 with discussion (NSF PAR copy)](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10170901)
- **Wilkinson (proposer of the vote of thanks).**
  - Coalescence ≠ convergence; "k represents 'wasted' computation".
  - "When the correction term is present, it can sometimes be large".
  - There is "little theory providing reassurance" for coupled Gibbs. His AR(1) example has a long-tailed coupling time (mean ≈ 2600, max 67,000).
  - "The debiasing does not eliminate burn-in—it just corrects for it". Amdahl's law `SpeedUp(N) = (b+n)/(b + n/N) → (b+n)/b` as N → ∞ (for example a limit of 10 for n = 9b), so parallel chains may be "an increasingly inefficient proposition for large numbers of processors (greater than of the order of 10²)".
  - Source: [JRSSB discussion (NSF PAR copy)](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10170901)
- **Sherlock (seconder).** The bias correction "is potentially very noisy", with `var(BC_{k:m} | τ) = O{(τ−k)³/(m−k)²}`. In a 20-d example the MSE is prohibitive until k passes the 0.999 quantile. He proposes Rao–Blackwellisation over coupling events (gains of up to 30×) and stresses reflection-maximal coupling (τ roughly linear rather than exponential in d) — [JRSSB discussion (NSF PAR copy)](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10170901)
- **Craiu & Meng.** Rewriting `H_k = h(X_{(τ−1)∨k}) + Σ_{j=k}^{τ−1} {h(X_j) − h(Y_j)}` exposes zero-mean differences Δ_j = h(X_j) − h(Y_j). These give control variates `C_k(η) = Σ_{j=k}^{η−2} Δ_j` with η the median of τ − ξ, ξ ~ Bernoulli(0.5). Gains were small when τ was small and vanished with time-averaging in the pump example, which led to "Double Happiness" (improved TV bounds) — [JRSSB discussion (NSF PAR copy)](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10170901)
- **Lee, Singh & Vihola.** Scalability in dimension is the key question. For coupled conditional backward-sampling particle filters, log C_T = O(T), k ≥ cT suffices, and the minimal parallel wall time is O(T² + T log P) — [JRSSB discussion (NSF PAR copy)](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10170901)
- **Gerber & Lee.** A controlled-variance (sub-maximal) Thorisson coupling, and reparameterisation via Bernstein–von Mises whitening — [JRSSB discussion (NSF PAR copy)](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10170901)
- **Chopin.** The choice among couplings of Gibbs steps matters (latent versus residual versus optimal-transport coupling). He asks about parameterisation effects and transport couplings that never produce equal outputs — [JRSSB discussion (NSF PAR copy)](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10170901)
- **Gu.** Latent-variable or missing-data models make E[τ] "extremely large"; an extension is needed — [JRSSB discussion (NSF PAR copy)](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10170901)
- **Paulin.** Do couplings with good dimension dependence exist for MALA, HMC and Gibbs? — [JRSSB discussion (NSF PAR copy)](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10170901)
- **Pompe & Holmes.** Applications to the posterior bootstrap and cut models. They ask for unbiased versions of *adaptive* MCMC, especially in two-stage procedures with N₁ different kernels to tune — [JRSSB discussion (NSF PAR copy)](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10170901)
- **Robert, Clarté, Hairault, Lawless & Ryder.** "Unbiasedness is not the ultimate goal… the paper may otherwise give the impression of achieving a 'free-lunch' result". The variance of the unbiased estimator "is at least the variance of the original MCMC estimator" (their claim) — [JRSSB discussion (NSF PAR copy)](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10170901)
- **Ryder, Clarté, Hairault, Lawless & Robert.** Initialise different pairs from different distributions and use mixture-based initialisation so that X and Y start apart. A large E[τ] is a useful warning — [JRSSB discussion (NSF PAR copy)](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10170901)
- **South, Nemeth & Oates.** Gradient-based (Stein) control variates fitted on half of the replicates. Median variance reduction ≈ 20× on the 302-dimensional logistic regression of Heng & Jacob (k = 330, m = 3300, R = 32) — [JRSSB discussion (NSF PAR copy)](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10170901); preprint [arXiv:1912.10496](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.10496)
- **Chak, Kantas & Pavliotis.** Correlate pairs (antithetic-style) for variance reduction, extending Nüsken & Pavliotis — [discussion PDF](https://www.ma.imperial.ac.uk/~nkantas/discussion_umcmc.pdf)
- **Vanetti & Doucet.** The lagged estimator (now standard), and sharing chains between pairs, which is equivalent to one long chain with lag RL and close to Neal's circular coupling — [JRSSB discussion (NSF PAR copy)](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10170901)
- **Vats & Jones.** Accept–reject samplers with linchpin variables can give one exact draw, making plain MCMC unbiased; "in settings where direct sampling is inefficient, the unbiased estimation techniques… are promising" — [JRSSB discussion (NSF PAR copy)](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10170901)
- **Other contributions.**
  - Middleton, Deligiannidis & Doucet: coupled PIMH with P(τ = 1) ≥ ½ and conditionally geometric τ; no truncation variable is needed.
  - Wang, Pollock, Roberts & Steinsaltz: Restore regenerative sampler towards perfect sampling.
  - Jasra, Law & Heng: four-way couplings for unbiased multilevel Monte Carlo.
  - Chai: terminology, and credible versus confidence intervals.
  - Source: [JRSSB discussion (NSF PAR copy)](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10170901)
- **The authors' reply.**
  - Parallel goals: their estimators give a "(2α, T)" gain (double precision at fixed time) in most settings. A "(α, T/2)" gain (same precision in half the time) is realistic only when the starting budget is moderate or large, which reconciles Amdahl's law with Glynn–Heidelberger CLTs.
  - Calls for theory on meeting times.
  - Endorses lags, control variates, Rao–Blackwellisation and antithetic ideas.
  - CRN as the default starting point for couplings.
  - Parameterisation matters.
  - Adaptive MCMC remains open.
  - Heterogeneous π0 gives non-identically distributed estimators.
  - L > 1 is safer.
  - It is "unclear whether the machinery of this paper bring us any closer to perfect sampling".
  - Source: [JRSSB reply (NSF PAR copy)](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10170901)
- **Later commentary.**
  - Craiu & Meng's handbook chapter (2024 preprint) presents unbiased MCMC as "one such instance of a sensible balance" between perfection (perfect sampling) and practicality. Their improved TV bound is "always tighter… but difference is of practical significance only when τ_L has a small median" — [Craiu & Meng, "Perfecting MCMC Sampling: Recipes and Reservations", arXiv:2401.02518](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.02518)
  - The 2024 review's open questions: "There is no guarantee that such ad hoc constructions can always be found"; coupling inhomogeneous or adaptive transitions "remains elusive"; negative estimates of positive quantities may have no general solution — [Atchadé & Jacob 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.06851)
  - Nested R̂ authors: kernels that couple quickly are not always available (for example HMC with many leapfrog steps) — [Margossian et al., arXiv:2110.13017](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.13017)

### Inferences
- The critiques that matter most for a non-expert-facing package are the silent ones: heavy tails and multimodality that look fine at finite R; repeated burn-in limiting gains from very many cores; and the absence of adaptive coupling, which means users cannot rely on within-run adaptation as they do in emcee or Stan. `telescopic` should design its UX around these: warnings, pilot-then-freeze tuning, and budget-aware parallel execution.
- Variance-reduction add-ons proposed in the discussion are proven to keep unbiasedness and are cheap to implement on stored chains: lags (already standard), Δ_j control variates, sample-split Stein control variates, and Rao–Blackwellised MH coupling probabilities. They are natural candidates for optional features after the core estimator.
